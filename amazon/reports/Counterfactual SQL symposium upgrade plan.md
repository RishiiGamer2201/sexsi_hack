# Lead with the audit, then seed the null

The best way to strengthen the counterfactual-database (CF) reward paper by Nov 30 is to make the existing evidence harder to dismiss, not to look for a new headline. Concretely: get three seeds per arm in the main pair by evaluating the seed-1 adapters stranded on the `sqlcf-l4` disk and training one more seed (about 10–11 L4 GPU-hours, about $10–15). Then commit five CPU-only analyses that turn the null into a mechanism. In the main runs only 27 of 240 GRPO groups carried any gradient, about 77% of the training copies could not kill a single mutant, and in the curriculum pair the single-DB arm's lucky-pass share rose from 0.59 to 0.73 while the strict CF arm stayed flat. Add the baselines a reviewer will demand (one larger database, a test-suite reward, a held-out bug family, a real-schema Spider audit), fix seven framing errors in the draft, and bring the public Hugging Face (HF) and GitHub record in line with what the paper claims. One correction comes before any of this. In its single seed, the main-pair CF arm finished **3.7 points below** the single-DB arm (−3.7 [−5.3, −2.3]). So neither the paper nor the poster may say the safer reward "costs nothing in accuracy", although one of the research notes proposes exactly that line. On novelty: ReViSQL already showed that execution rewards in text-to-SQL RL produce false positives, but nobody has yet trained on rewards graded across hidden, code-generated CF databases with answer-preserving controls. That is our claim. The window from December to Feb 18 is for a positive result: a deliberately exploitable "LeakySQL" environment, five seeds, a corrected-BIRD audit checked by the SpotIt verifier, and a pip-installable audit tool behind a "fool the reward" demo. That costs about $100 for the core tier and up to $300 extended, and it should wait for the Dec 31 acceptance. A live check on Nov 21 found the AWS account has **0 vCPU of GPU (G/VT) quota** in every region checked, so the team's GCP L4 is the only GPU before Nov 30. None of the paid steps can start until the user approves GPU spend, decides on Bedrock and an AWS quota request (ideally after moving the CLI off root credentials), supplies author affiliations and emails, and picks a code licence.

## Nine days buy robustness, not a new headline

The project already has the evidence to fill four pages. It has 14 experiment families, every result reproduced by `verify_all.py`, and a draft whose 41 auto-checked numbers all match `results/` ([internal audit](../research_notes/Counterfactual%20SQL%20symposium%20upgrade%20plan/internal_repo_audit.md); [PROJECT_REPORT §5](../PROJECT_REPORT.md)). The audit result is the paper's strongest claim. On 1,302 synthetic items, blind CF copies cut the share of **buggy mutants paid in full from 29.5% to 16.8%** and of **constructed hacks from 54.1% to 1.9%**, while rejecting **0 of 2,847 correct queries**. The same audit found an AgentTune exploit (`WHERE 1 = 1` padding scores like honest SQL), and the maintainers fixed it upstream the next day ([main.tex](../paper/iteration0/main.tex)). The weak points are just as clear. There is one seed per arm. The training result is a null, and in the main pair a negative one: verified accuracy 9.0% against 12.7%. The curriculum pair gives −0.8 [−1.8, +0.2]. All training and testing use one synthetic schema, with Spider as the only real data. The Spider transfer gain of +1.9 points does not survive McNemar (p = 0.14 and 0.94), and its bootstrap treats the four samples per question as independent ([main.tex](../paper/iteration0/main.tex)).

Reviewers will probe three questions: "is this just more test data?", "your bugs and copies are your own", and "one run proves nothing". Each has a cheap answer. Most of the experiments recommended earlier but never run (B4 open specialists, a held-out-family audit, family-blind copies, NL2SQL-BUGs, BIRD copies, the advantage-sign replay) were dropped for time, L4 stockouts and the Nov 11 Lexsi-only scoping, not because they failed ([internal audit §2](../research_notes/Counterfactual%20SQL%20symposium%20upgrade%20plan/internal_repo_audit.md)). Most need only CPU time. New training variants, by contrast, cannot be run with enough seeds to mean anything before Nov 30. The plan therefore spends Horizon A on seeds, analyses, baselines and fixes, and saves the experiment that could change the headline for Horizon B.

One framing rule overrides everything else. The audience research recommends the pitch "the safer reward costs nothing in accuracy" ([audience notes §3](../research_notes/Counterfactual%20SQL%20symposium%20upgrade%20plan/amazon_audience_presentation.md)). The data do not support it. The honest sentence is: "In one seed the CF-mean arm scored 3.7 points below the single-DB arm in domain; in the strict curriculum pair the difference was −0.8 with a CI that includes zero; seeds 1–2 show [result]." If the three-seed mean puts the gap inside noise, "no detectable accuracy cost at three seeds" becomes available. Until then it is not.

## Five zero-GPU analyses already explain the null

The literature now says when a stricter verifier should change RL outcomes, and our setting meets none of the conditions. RLVR tolerates unstructured verifier noise well. Plesner et al. found that up to 20% noise stays within 1 point of the clean baseline on MBPP and that "the structure of the noise matters more than the aggregate error rate" ([arXiv 2604.07666](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.07666)). Lv et al. report Qwen-2.5-7B reaching 72% with 40% of rewards flipped, against 75% clean ([arXiv 2505.22653](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.22653)). What hurts is systematic, learnable false positives, which Egashira et al. show can cause plateaus or collapse depending on the error pattern rather than the rate ([arXiv 2605.02909](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.02909)). The 58-study synthesis by Gupta and Joshi reaches the same conclusion ([arXiv 2610.17824](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.17824)). Our closest analogue, Helff et al.'s extensional versus isomorphic verifier, opened a hacking gap only **around step 250**, on a 7B reasoning model running about 500 steps on 64 H100s ([arXiv 2604.15149](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.15149)). We ran 60 steps on a 3.35B model, and only 8% of passes were lucky, all of them honest semantic bugs in the hand audit. The direct code-domain twin of our result is Zhang's preregistered MBPP study. Hardening the reward suite changed held-out accuracy by 0.20 points (one-sided upper bound 0.75), and the paper framed the outcome as "hardening the reward removes the measurement inflation, though here it buys little capability" ([arXiv 2607.11022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.11022)). Citing it makes our null expected rather than anomalous.

Our own logs supply the mechanism. A read-only pass over `runs/grpo_*_rewards.jsonl` (in a scratchpad script that must be committed and added to `verify_all.py` before any number reaches the paper) gives four results ([internal audit §3](../research_notes/Counterfactual%20SQL%20symposium%20upgrade%20plan/internal_repo_audit.md)):

| Analysis | Finding | Use in paper |
|---|---|---|
| Advantage replay (groups of 6) | Main runs: only **27 of 240 groups** carried any gradient under single-DB reward. CF-mean, strict and penalty forms would flip the advantage sign of **3.6%, 5.3% and 5.6% of rollouts**. In the curriculum, penalty or mean would have revived about twice as many groups (105–107 vs 53) | Replaces "rewards disagreed on 7%" with a gradient-level dose statement |
| Effective copies | 442 of 445 GRPO prompts had **2 copies, not 3**. Only **202 of 893 chosen copies** kill any base-blind mutant, and tier copies on F1–F3 questions kill none. `_select` fills the preserving slot even when the copy kills nothing | A code-level cause of the weak signal, plus an obvious fix (a "require kill" flag) |
| Monitor curve (R̃−R gap per step) | Main pair: B 0.104→0.075, C 0.133→0.058. **Curriculum: B_cur 0.588→0.729 (rising), Cmin_cur 0.529→0.533 (flat)** | The only training-time divergence evidence. Turns the "monitor the gap" advice into a figure |
| Per-family audit | Blind-CF mutant pay F1 8.5%, F2 20.2%, F3 23.3%, F4 17.0%, against 22–36% for single-DB. Hacks paid 0–4.1% vs 50–59% | Appendix table, already in `e0_reward_audit.json` |

The curriculum curve needs a careful caption. It is one seed, the rise sits in the arm trained on lucky-prone prompts, and the grouping-by-6 assumption behind the replay has not been checked against TRL's sampler order (the logs carry no item IDs). It is still the most persuasive picture the project has: under the single-DB reward, the policy produced a growing share of answers that a hidden copy rejects, and the CF reward held that share flat. The treatment-dose arithmetic belongs next to it. In a group with five honest passes and one lucky pass, the single-DB reward gives zero gradient because all six rewards are 1. Under the CF reward the lucky rollout gets an advantage of about **−2.2** and the others about +0.45 ([training notes Q1](../research_notes/Counterfactual%20SQL%20symposium%20upgrade%20plan/training_signal_methodology.md)). The per-instance signal is strong, but it applies to only about 100 of 1,440 rollouts. That dose is too small to show up on a 331-item test.

For Horizon B, the levers that change this are ranked by expected effect per GPU-hour. First, a disagreement-mined pool of 100–200 prompts where SFT samples include both CF-verified and lucky passes. This should raise the share of disagreeing rollouts from about 7% toward 30–50%. Second, DAPO-style dynamic sampling, which drops zero-variance groups ([arXiv 2503.14476](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.14476)), applied to both arms. It needs custom code, because TRL does not support it. Third, groups of 12–16 instead of 6. Fourth, copies filtered to those that kill at least one mutant. Fifth, a lucky-pass penalty of −0.5, monitored for refusals and empty SQL. Unanchored partial credit should stay out, since our own E11 showed it pays mutants 0.65 against 0.30 ([main.tex appendix](../paper/iteration0/main.tex)). Keep std normalisation in the CF arm on purpose: Dr. GRPO's removal of it ([arXiv 2503.20783](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.20783)) would shrink the −2.2 lucky-pass signal.

The experiment most likely to produce a positive result is a model organism, not a better natural run. In a **LeakySQL** environment, D0 is shrunk to 1–3 rows per table so that hard-coding literals seen while exploring reproduces the gold result. The copies keep the schema and change the contents. The arms are single-DB versus strict CF, with an optional third arm whose SFT data is "hack-seeded" with 5–10% hard-coded trajectories. That follows Anthropic and Redwood, who first gave the model knowledge of the hack and then trained in hackable environments ([arXiv 2511.18397](https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.18397)). ImpossibleBench provides the framing for a deliberately exploitable benchmark ([arXiv 2510.20270](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.20270)). The predicted outcome is that single-DB training reward rises while the hard-coding rate climbs and CF accuracy stays flat, while the CF arm's hard-coding rate stays near zero. Paired with the natural-run null, the two results say *when* a CF reward matters. The cost is about 20–60 L4 GPU-hours for 2–3 arms × 2–3 seeds. That is too risky to make the main Nov 30 result, but a one-seed pilot is a reasonable stretch goal if the seeds finish early.

## Three seeds and a named test turn one run into a result

A second seed exists but has not been evaluated. The B_s1 and C_s1 adapters live only on the stopped VM disk. B_s1 finished 60/60 steps, and C_s1 is presumed but not logged as finished ([PROJECT_REPORT §5](../PROJECT_REPORT.md)). "Seed" in this setup means prompt order plus the torch RNG, because TRL fixes the colocated vLLM engine seed to the process index ([scripts/vm_seed1.sh](../../scripts/vm_seed1.sh)). The paper must say so, or a reviewer will read the seeds as fully independent runs. The VM session should, in order: check C_s1 and any `checkpoint-20/40` directories; copy all adapters off the disk; evaluate B_s1 and C_s1 on the 331×8 test and on Spider; train seed 2 of both arms; evaluate seed 2; then stop the VM. That gives three seeds per arm for the main pair.

Statistics should follow published guidance, and the primary test should be named in advance. Agarwal et al. show that point estimates from a few runs are unreliable and recommend stratified bootstrap CIs and probability of improvement ([arXiv 2108.13264](https://arxiv.org/abs/2108.13264)). Hochlehnert et al. found that seeds and small implementation choices swing LM-reasoning RL results by double digits and that many claimed gains were not significant ([arXiv 2504.07086](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.07086)). Miller recommends paired differences with clustered standard errors ([arXiv 2411.00640](https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.00640)). The primary test should be a hierarchical paired bootstrap: resample seeds within each arm, then resample items, clustered by question on the test set and by database for Spider's roughly 20 dev databases. Per-seed exact McNemar tests serve as a secondary check. Every seed gets its own table row, and Holm correction applies across the in-domain and Spider endpoints. All existing results are post hoc and must be labelled that way.

Power arithmetic should appear in the paper. With about 10% discordant items, detecting a 2-point paired difference at α = 0.05 and 80% power needs about **1,960 items**. One Spider dev pass (1,034 questions) is underpowered, and the paper should report the minimum detectable effect rather than imply a test was decisive ([training notes Q4](../research_notes/Counterfactual%20SQL%20symposium%20upgrade%20plan/training_signal_methodology.md)). An equivalence framing helps, but only where it holds. The curriculum CI [−1.8, +0.2] supports "CF did not improve in-domain accuracy by more than 0.2 points". It does *not* pass a ±1-point TOST, because its lower bound is −1.8. For Horizon B, a git-tagged `prereg.md` committed before any new run should fix the primary endpoint, the contrast, the seeds, the step count, the stopping rule and a non-inferiority margin, the design Zhang used for MBPP ([arXiv 2607.11022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.11022)).

## Reviewers will ask for bigger databases, test suites, judges and an attacker that moves second

The audit currently compares the CF reward only with single-DB and set-comparison rewards. Two CPU-only baselines answer "isn't this just more data?" before anyone asks. The first is a **single larger database** at 10× and 100× scale, plus a compute-matched variant whose total rows equal the original plus k copies. Our own size sweep already shows mutant pass rates falling from 29.2% at 50 customers to 0.24% at 5,000 ([main.tex appendix](../paper/iteration0/main.tex)), so the honest comparison is at equal total size. If a 5,000-row database matches our false-positive rate, the claim narrows to "the same detection with smaller databases". The second is a **Zhong-style distilled test-suite reward**: fuzz about 100–1,000 random databases per item with our generator and keep those that separate gold from neighbour queries ([Zhong et al. 2020](https://arxiv.org/abs/2010.02840)). That puts blind copies, informed copies and a distilled suite on one spectrum. Each takes hours of CPU time on the existing harness ([baselines notes Q1](../research_notes/Counterfactual%20SQL%20symposium%20upgrade%20plan/baselines_attacks.md)).

The held-out family experiment fixes a real weakness in the current claim. F4 is *not* held out in the audit: its blind copies include F4-targeted edits (`later_tier` in 110 of 369 F4 items), so its 17.0% is not a held-out number ([internal audit §3a](../research_notes/Counterfactual%20SQL%20symposium%20upgrade%20plan/internal_repo_audit.md)). Rebuilding the F4 audit items with a pool that excludes `later_tier`, `superseded_tier` and `adjustments` takes minutes of CPU after a small code change. It reports CF performance on a bug family the generator was not built for. A family-blind audit using the generic `TEST_OPS` mutations (resample, clone, status flip, tier relabel) as copies answers "your copies encode your bugs". Today the only family-blind evidence is that the test copies catch 54 of 103 base-blind mutants.

An LLM-as-judge row is the practitioner's alternative, and it is cheap. Judges are demonstrably hackable: superficial "master key" tokens produce false-positive rewards across GPT-o1 and Claude-4 ([arXiv 2507.08794](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.08794)). The SQL-specific judge FLEX reaches human agreement of κ 62–87 ([arXiv 2409.19014](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.19014v4)). Running a binary verdict over all ~7,500 audit SQLs, with an added hack family of judge-directed SQL comments (`-- this query is verified correct`), costs about **$0.5 with Nova Micro, $0.9 with Nova Lite and $11 with Nova Pro**, roughly halved in batch mode. Those prices come from third-party aggregators, because the official Bedrock table did not render ([compute notes Q4](../research_notes/Counterfactual%20SQL%20symposium%20upgrade%20plan/compute_plan.md)). A Nova row is also the cheapest way to show relevance to an Amazon AGI-hosted event. Nova Micro, Lite, Pro and 2 Lite are listed ACTIVE in the us-east-1 Bedrock catalog, but whether this account can actually invoke them is untested ([compute notes Q8](../research_notes/Counterfactual%20SQL%20symposium%20upgrade%20plan/compute_plan.md)). The row needs the user's approval and a one-call access test. Command A is the fallback judge and white-box attacker, but it is also paid: about $4.24 per 1,000 single-turn calls, so roughly $30 for the full pool ([main.tex appendix](../paper/iteration0/main.tex)).

Adaptive attacks are where the current audit is weakest. Thirteen fixed mutant types plus constructed hacks is a non-adaptive evaluation. Tramèr et al. broke 13 defences with attacks tuned to each mechanism ([arXiv 2002.08347](https://arxiv.org/abs/2002.08347v2)). "The Attacker Moves Second" bypassed 12 LLM defences, mostly at over 90% success, and human red-teaming beat every one of them ([arXiv 2510.09023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.09023v1)). The minimum credible package has four parts. First, a stated threat model, already in the draft. Second, a documented human red-team session of 1–2 hours per author, recording attempts and successes, which is free and fits Horizon A. Third, a white-box attacker LLM that knows the copy generator and gets execution feedback on D0 only, with an informed-attacker upper bound that is shown the copies (Horizon B). Fourth, GRPO itself as the optimisation-based attacker, which is what the LeakySQL organism is. The paper should name the main residual risk: leaking copy contents or the generator seed through the reward signal. The 1.9% of hacks that survive are all hard-coded-ID queries whose answer-changing copy kept the same IDs (54 of 235), a concrete generator fix to make before Horizon B.

Policy baselines belong in Horizon A only as prompted runs. Qwen2.5-Coder-3B and Llama-3.2-3B (size-matched), plus OmniSQL-7B and Arctic-Text2SQL-R1-7B (specialist ceilings), on the 331-item test set take minutes each with vLLM on the L4. The useful number is each model's gap between single-DB EX and CF-verified accuracy. Arctic is trained on an execution-only reward and its authors say finer-grained rewards "often backfired" ([arXiv 2505.20315](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.20315v2)), so a large gap for Arctic would support the thesis directly. Retraining a second model family with both rewards waits for Horizon B. It answers the Spurious Rewards warning that RLVR effects can be family-specific ([arXiv 2506.10947](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.10947)), and Tiny Aya being non-Qwen is a point in our favour.

## Real schemas: Spider now, corrected BIRD next

The cheapest real-data addition uses files already on disk. `external/testsuite_db` (Zhong's Spider dev suites) and `external/ESMp` (gold queries plus eight systems' predictions) support two new analyses. One mutates Spider-dev gold SQL with generic operators (drop DISTINCT, INNER↔LEFT, drop a WHERE conjunct, COUNT(*)↔COUNT(DISTINCT)) and compares single-DB with suite pay rates. The other scores the stored `spider_*.jsonl` trajectories by question type ([internal audit §3i](../research_notes/Counterfactual%20SQL%20symposium%20upgrade%20plan/internal_repo_audit.md)). The benchmark to compare against is Zhong's: single-database checking has a **6.5% mean false-positive rate** (maximum 9.0%) across 21 Spider dev submissions, rising to 11.0% on extra-hard questions ([arXiv 2010.02840](https://arxiv.org/abs/2010.02840)). Our existing 8.1–14.9% lucky shares for eight published systems already sit near that. The paper must be clear that Spider with Zhong suites tests the idea of multi-database grading on real schemas, not our generator.

The real test of the generator is BIRD, and it needs one to two developer-days of code. The reward layer (`rewards.py`, `compare.py`) is already schema-agnostic. The copy generator `cf.py` is hard-wired to the retail schema through positional tuple indices and domain helpers ([internal audit §4](../research_notes/Counterfactual%20SQL%20symposium%20upgrade%20plan/internal_repo_audit.md)). A new generic module would introspect foreign keys with `PRAGMA` and apply family-blind edits to the rows the gold query touches: fan-out duplicates behind foreign keys, orphan parents, NULL injection, boundary rows at each gold constant, ties at `ORDER BY … LIMIT`, case variants of string literals, and random subsampling ([real-data notes Q2](../research_notes/Counterfactual%20SQL%20symposium%20upgrade%20plan/real_data_counterfactuals.md)). Running Zhong's mechanical neighbour operators through sqlglot then gives a coverage metric that rebuts "you wrote the bug families", because someone else's published operators generate the neighbours.

BIRD's gold quality decides which subset to use. An expert audit found **52.8% of BIRD Mini-Dev examples erroneous** ([arXiv 2601.08778](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.08778v3)). In SpotIt's manual review of 50 EX-pass/verifier-fail discrepancies, only **26% were genuine prediction errors** and about 10% were ambiguous; most of the rest traced to bad gold ([arXiv 2510.26840](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.26840v2)). A CF reward amplifies gold bugs, so on raw BIRD its flags are an upper bound on false accepts and double as a gold-bug detector. The plan should use the uiuc-kang-lab corrected 100-question dev subset, which ships 16 leaderboard agents' predictions on both original and corrected gold, so generation costs nothing. Arcwise-Plat-SQL (corrected Mini-Dev, CC-BY-SA-4.0) comes second ([uiuc-kang-lab repo](https://github.com/uiuc-kang-lab/text_to_sql_benchmarks)). SpotIt is the independent oracle: public code, about 1–4 s per pair on BIRD dev, CPU only, with no window functions or recursive CTEs ([arXiv 2510.26840](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.26840v2)). On BIRD dev it cut 10 systems' accuracy by 9.8–13.5 points. The target table has these columns: EX, CF-EX, oracle-EX, false-accept and false-reject per reward, the CF–oracle kappa, and a manually adjudicated split of 50–100 flags into genuine error, ambiguous and gold error, run on both original and corrected gold. One check must come first. If BIRD's evaluator compares results as sets, duplicate-sensitive families are invisible to both rewards unless ours keeps bag comparison.

This belongs in Horizon B, with a Horizon A stretch goal of running the generic generator on the 100-question subset if it is working by Nov 26. Spider2-lite (135 SQLite questions) is a later stretch goal. SpotIt supports only 51–61% of its pairs, mostly because of window functions. Our execution-based copies handle window functions, which is a selling point.

## ReViSQL owns the problem; the hidden-copy reward is still unclaimed

ReViSQL is the paper reviewers will hold ours against. Over 300 pilot steps on Kimi-K2.6, VeriEQL flagged **28.8–38.4% (32.8% on average) of positive execution rewards** as false positives, and the rate did not fall during training. Its reward pays 0.8 instead of 1 when the result matches but VeriEQL refutes the query, and it passes timeouts and unsupported queries at full reward ([arXiv 2603.20004](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.20004)). We therefore cannot claim to have discovered false-positive SQL rewards, and the paper should present our 29.5% mutant and 54.1% hack pay rates as consistent with and complementary to ReViSQL's 32.8%. The differences are precise. ReViSQL executes only on the example database, uses a symbolic verifier with a soft downweight, reports no verifier-only ablation, and gets its gains mainly from corrected data at 235B scale. Our reward executes on hidden, code-generated databases organised by bug family, includes answer-preserving controls, handles SQL that VeriEQL does not support, and isolates the reward in a single-variable ablation.

The two other close neighbours each share one ingredient. SynSQL grades on LLM-synthesised databases and finds 3–14% drops, but only for evaluation ([arXiv 2604.27261](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.27261)). Helff et al.'s isomorphic perturbation verifier removes shortcuts as a training reward, but in inductive-logic tasks, with answer-preserving relabelings only ([arXiv 2604.15149](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.15149)). Progress-SQL uses augmented-database test suites only for evaluation ([arXiv 2606.06825](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.06825v1)), and SQLWitness generates counterexample databases for refutation at 0.12 s per pair, not as a reward ([arXiv 2610.22442](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.22442)). The defensible claim, phrased "to our knowledge", is a training-time text-to-SQL reward that pays only when results match on the original database and on hidden, code-generated CF databases targeting specific bug families, with answer-preserving controls and an audit before training. Two claims must stay out: "first to find false-positive SQL rewards" (ReViSQL) and "first multi-database SQL grading" (Zhong 2020, XData, SynSQL). The related-work paragraph should add Helff, Zhang's MBPP study, SynSQL, SpotIt, SQLWitness, DualSQL and Progress-SQL. arXiv postings from late November may not be indexed yet, so a fresh search for "counterfactual database" + SQL + RL should run on Nov 28 ([related-work notes Q4](../research_notes/Counterfactual%20SQL%20symposium%20upgrade%20plan/related_work_novelty.md)).

For the Amazon panel, position the reward as differential testing on a verification spectrum. Single-DB EX is weakest, then CF databases (ours: solver-free and fast enough for RL), then bounded SMT equivalence (SpotIt), then a full equivalence proof. That bridges to Amazon's automated-reasoning theme without claiming formal methods. Byron Cook argues that "the definition of good and bad behavior is a specification" and calls for combining automated-reasoning tools with RL ([All Things Distributed](https://allthingsdistributed.com/2026/02/a-chat-with-byron-cook-on-automated-reasoning-and-trust-in-ai-systems.html)). Amazon's own SQLENS detects semantic errors in LLM-generated SQL ([Amazon Science](https://www.amazon.science/publications/sqlens-an-end-to-end-framework-for-error-detection-and-correction-in-text-to-sql)). Bedrock Knowledge Bases expose a standalone `GenerateQuery` API that returns SQL without executing it ([AWS docs](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/bedrock/latest/userguide/knowledge-base-build-structured.html)), which makes it a natural future audit target. In the intro, one sentence on "verifying the code an agent writes before paying it reward" and one on NIST CAISI's point that "scoring functions must capture the evaluator's intent" ([NIST CAISI](https://www.nist.gov/caissi/1-background-ai-models-can-cheat-evaluations)) do more than a section would. Say "in the spirit of" automated reasoning, never "integrates with".

## Conclusion

The project's value to a trusted-AI audience is that it measured the reward before trusting it, found a live exploit in a public library, and then reported honestly that a harder reward did not train a better agent at this scale. The new log analyses make that null explain itself. The reward changed the advantage sign on a few percent of rollouts, three-quarters of the training copies could not catch anything, and the one place where hacking pressure existed (the curriculum) is where the single-DB arm's lucky share started climbing while the CF arm's held still. That reframes the contribution from "CF rewards help" to "CF rewards are an inexpensive, auditable check whose training effect depends on whether the policy is being paid for bugs". It also gives a falsifiable prediction to test in January: in an environment where the exploit is learnable, the CF arm should suppress it.

The main risk is not reviewers missing the strength of the work but the team overstating it. The research notes contain two such traps, "no accuracy cost" and "first to find false-positive SQL rewards". Both are wrong, and either would cost more credibility than any missing experiment. Spending $25 on seeds and nothing on new training variants before Nov 30, then gating the larger budget on acceptance, keeps every claim in the submitted PDF defensible. It also keeps open the one experiment that could make the poster's headline positive.
