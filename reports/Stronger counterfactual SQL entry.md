# Make the counterfactual reward actually bite

> **Update (Nov 9, after the A1 replay on the logs):** the "partly lenient" hypothesis in the summary and the first section below is **refuted**. Rollouts that fail the original DB but pass a copy: 0.1% in B, 0.3% in C. Lucky rollouts: 7.1% in B, 7.2% in C. So the ~7% disagreement is lucky passes, and C was truly stricter. The arithmetic below assumed a low training pass rate, but the real original-DB pass rate on training prompts was ~86%. The training prompts were easy, so most groups carried no gradient. B3 (C-min "conditional on lenient") is therefore not motivated by leniency. The rest of the report stands.

The most valuable thing the team can do before Nov 14 costs no GPU time. First, replay the logged GRPO rewards to find out what the counterfactual (CF) reward actually changed. Simple arithmetic on the team's own numbers says the C reward was probably not a stricter version of B. Lucky passes are only ~8.3% of original-DB passes, so they can explain at most about 1–2 of the 7 points of B/C disagreement. The rest most likely comes from C's *mean* aggregation paying 0.2–0.8 to queries that fail the original DB but happen to match gold on one or more copies. Under GRPO's per-group normalisation, that turns otherwise dead all-fail groups into positive updates on wrong SQL. That would be a concrete, testable explanation for why C finished 3.7 points below B (95% CI [−5.3, −2.3], McNemar p < 0.001). The gap is real for these two checkpoints. Whether it is a reward effect or training-seed variance stays open until a second seed is run. Second, put the core claim on third-party data at zero cost: Spider's public distilled test-suite databases and published prediction files from about 12 systems let the team measure "lucky pass" rates of real models on a real benchmark. A second open RL reward (SkyRL-SQL) can be audited with the existing mutants. Third, spend under $10 of L4 time on one second seed per arm, plus a calibration run that finds a regime where the single-DB verifier is weak. Recent literature agrees that stricter verifiers matter mainly when false positives are a large share of positive rewards: ~33% in ReViSQL, versus ~8% here. The write-up should therefore lead with the reward-integrity audit and the real before→after gains (base 0.9% → SFT 9.4% → GRPO-B 12.7%). It should own the aggregator choice as a design lesson. It should then use the Nov 16–21 window for a pre-registered retest with a min or penalty reward on a lucky-prone curriculum, across 3+ seeds.

## The 7% disagreement hides a reward that was partly more lenient than B

The team's framing so far treats C as "B, but stricter". The logged numbers do not support that. A rollout can disagree between the two rewards in only two ways. In a *lucky* rollout, the query passes the original DB but fails at least one copy, so B = 1 and C < 1. In a *lenient* rollout, the query fails the original but matches gold on at least one copy, so B = 0 and C > 0. Lucky rollouts were 8.2–8.3% of original-DB passes in both runs. For lucky passes alone to make up 7% of all rollouts, the training-time original-DB pass rate would have to be about 84%. That is implausible for a policy that scores 10–13% on test. Even at a 30% training pass rate, lucky rollouts are about 2.5% of the total, which leaves **at least ~4.5 of the 7 disagreement points as lenient rollouts, where C paid partial credit to SQL that was wrong on the visible database**. This is derived arithmetic, not a measurement. The pass vectors in `runs/grpo_C_rewards.jsonl` settle it in minutes.

GRPO normalisation makes those lenient rollouts matter more than their count suggests. TRL's default `scale_rewards="group"` computes each advantage as (r − mean)/std within the group, and the docs cite Dr. GRPO's warning that per-group std scaling causes difficulty bias ([TRL GRPO docs](https://huggingface.co/docs/trl/main/en/grpo_trainer)). Std scaling is scale-invariant. A group of eight that is all-fail under B carries zero gradient. Under C-mean, if one wrong query scores 0.2 on a copy, the same group gives that query an advantage of +2.65, exactly what a genuine pass would get under B. At ~10% pass rates, an item with p = 0.1 is all-fail in about 43% of groups (0.9⁸). C-mean therefore had a large pool of dead groups to "revive" with updates toward wrong SQL. The same algebra shows that C-mean barely penalises genuine lucky passes. A group whose only pass is lucky gets identical advantages under B and C, and in mixed groups a lucky 0.2 still sits above the group mean. Code-RL practice warns against pass-fraction rewards for this reason. DeepCoder rejected K/N partial credit because it teaches models to game public tests, and paid only all-or-nothing outcomes ([Together AI](https://together.ai/blog/deepcoder)). Arctic-Text2SQL-R1 reports that finer-grained SQL rewards "induced 'lazy' behaviors" ([arXiv 2505.20315](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.20315v2)). The mean aggregator was the weakest choice available. *Min* (lucky = fail) or an explicit penalty (lucky < fail) is what the literature supports.

The second half of the mechanism is that 60 steps never reached the regime where a stricter verifier has something to fix. The lucky share of original-DB passes *fell* from ~11% to ~6% over training in both runs, so B's policy did not learn to exploit its weaker verifier. That fits Helff et al.'s isomorphic-perturbation study, the closest non-SQL analogue to answer-changing copies. With an extensional verifier, the hacking gap opened only around step 250 and reached ~3.5 of 10 reward points by step 500. With the perturbation verifier, it stayed near zero ([arXiv 2604.15149](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.15149)). ReViSQL, the closest SQL work, found that 28.8–38.4% of positive execution rewards on Kimi-K2.6 were VeriEQL-refuted false positives, and that this rate "does not decrease as training progresses" ([arXiv 2603.20004](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.20004)). A strong model generating many positives is where a stricter check moves advantages. A 3.35B model at ~10% accuracy generates few positives, and only ~8% of those are lucky. Razin et al. give the theory: optimisation speed depends on reward *variance under the current policy*, so "even a perfectly accurate reward model can lead to arbitrarily slow ground truth reward maximization" ([arXiv 2503.15477](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.15477)). Put together, this predicts a small difference between B and a strictly-stricter C. It does not predict C being worse. The lenient-credit hypothesis does, which is why the replay comes first.

One caution: the replay can show that the mechanism exists, not that it caused the gap. Only a second seed separates a reward effect from run variance. Small-model RLVR seed spreads of ~0.5–3 points are typical, and "A Sober Look at Progress in LM Reasoning" found many reported 1.5B RL gains within seed variance ([arXiv 2504.07086](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.07086)). The eval-level gap is significant across items (paired bootstrap, McNemar), but that test holds the two trained checkpoints fixed.

## Prior work brackets the method between test suites and solver verification

Judges with a text-to-SQL background will know Zhong, Yu and Klein's distilled test suites. That work found that a single database fails to distinguish ~5% of minimally mutated "neighbour" queries, while a suite built from many random databases distinguishes over 99% ([arXiv 2010.02840](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2010.02840)). It was built for evaluation, not reward. In 2025–2026 the frontier moved to formal search for a distinguishing database. SpotIt runs SMT-based bounded equivalence over all BIRD-dev pairs and cuts top-system accuracy by roughly 10–14 points. It moved the leaderboard leader from 1st to 4th, and its own manual review found that most disagreements were caused by bad gold SQL rather than bad predictions ([arXiv 2510.26840](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.26840v2)). VeriEQL, the engine behind it, refutes 96% of non-equivalent pairs with fewer than three tuples ([arXiv 2403.03193](https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.03193)). Gold noise is severe on public benchmarks: 52.8% of BIRD Mini-Dev instances carry annotation errors ([arXiv 2601.08778](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.08778)). That supports the team's choice to measure on generated databases with trusted gold. The write-up should say so explicitly.

Only two RL works put stricter execution checks inside the reward. **ReViSQL** pays 0.8 instead of 1 when results match but VeriEQL refutes, and gains 2.8–3.8 points at 235B scale. That gain is confounded with process-reward terms, and ReViSQL reports that clean training data mattered far more (13.9–17.1 points) ([arXiv 2603.20004](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.20004)). **DualSQL**, published in September 2026, trained Qwen3-8B on a duplicate- and order-aware matcher (REX). It gained +3.0 on the strict metric but only +0.4 on plain EX ([arXiv 2609.18135](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.18135v1)). That is the same "stricter reward, near-null headline" pattern the team sees, and citing it makes the null look expected rather than anomalous. An unreviewed repo, rl-sql, scores RL rewards across three seeded instances per schema for exactly the lucky-pass reason ([GitHub](https://github.com/devChaudhari7/rl-sql)). The defensible novelty claim is narrow and real. The method is solver-free and bug-family-targeted. It uses answer-changing and answer-preserving copies, works for any SQL the engine runs (including window functions, which SpotIt does not support), and is used as a hard RL reward on a 3B model with a single-variable ablation. The team should not claim to be the first to verify SQL on multiple databases.

The general RLVR literature explains why reward precision rarely shows up at this scale. Spurious Rewards found that random rewards recovered 21.4 of Qwen2.5-Math-7B's 29.1-point gain, though not for Llama or OLMo. The authors recommend spurious-reward controls as standard diagnostics ([PMLR v306](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v306/shao26d.html)). Lv et al. found that math RLVR tolerates 40% flipped rewards before collapsing ([arXiv 2505.22653](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.22653)), and 7% disagreement is far below that threshold. Huang et al. state the point the audit should lean on: "classification accuracy of a verifier does not necessarily reflect its resistance to reward hacking" ([arXiv 2505.22203](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.22203v2)). The team's audit measures hackability directly. Single-DB rewards pay 54.1% of hacks and 29.5% of gold-mutants. Copies cut these to 1.9% and 16.8%, with 0 of 2,847 correct queries rejected. That is the axis where the contribution is strongest.

## Zero-GPU experiments can move the evidence onto real benchmarks

The judges' obvious question is "does this happen with real models on real benchmarks?" The answer is already downloadable. `taoyds/test-suite-sql-eval` (Apache-2.0) ships distilled test-suite databases for Spider and ten other datasets, with an evaluator that runs plain EX or test-suite execution from one CLI ([GitHub](https://github.com/taoyds/test-suite-sql-eval)). The ESM+ repository ships Spider-dev prediction files for nine systems, including GPT-4 Turbo, Claude 3 Opus, DAIL-SQL and DIN-SQL ([GitHub](https://github.com/bossben/ESMp)). OmniSQL publishes greedy and multi-sample predictions for its 7B, 14B and 32B models on Spider and BIRD ([OmniSQL predictions](https://github.com/RUCKBReasoning/OmniSQL/blob/main/OmniSQL_prediction_results/README.md)). Scoring each file on the original database and on the suite gives a per-system lucky-pass rate, #(EX pass ∧ TS fail)/#(EX pass), on CPU in a few hours. ESM+ already reports that even test-suite execution keeps 10–23% false positives against a stricter structural judge, with prompted LLM systems worse than fine-tuned ones ([arXiv 2407.07313](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2407.07313)). The team's own measurement adds a cleaner single-DB-versus-many-DB delta. Either outcome is useful. Rates comparable to the team's synthetic numbers generalise the claim. Rates near zero show that Spider's dense databases mask the problem, and that is exactly the slice-size effect the team found (29% invisible bugs at 50 customers vs <1% at 500), now visible on a public benchmark.

A second widely used reward can be audited for free. SkyRL-SQL's `calculate_reward_single` executes predicted and gold SQL on one database. It compares `frozenset`s of rows, which makes it blind to row order and duplicates, and it pays 1.0 on a match ([SkyRL utils.py](https://github.com/NovaSky-AI/SkyRL/blob/main/skyrl-gym/skyrl_gym/envs/sql/utils.py)). Running the existing mutants and hacks through it, then through `min` over the copies, reproduces the AgentTune finding on a second stack. It also separates two independent holes. The *database* hole is the one counterfactual copies close. The *comparator* hole covers dropped DISTINCT, dropped ORDER BY and duplicated rows, which are paid on every database under set comparison. Only a multiset or ordered comparator closes it, as DualSQL's REX does. Quantifying both holes makes the Problem #9 ("reward-hacking defences") story complete rather than partial.

The stored samples also allow a practically useful test-time result. LEVER found that execution-based majority voting does nothing for SQL on one database (Spider dev 75.3 greedy vs 75.2 MBR-exec), while a learned verifier reached 81.9 ([arXiv 2302.08468](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2302.08468)). Arctic reports +0.9 points from 8-way voting at 7B ([arXiv 2505.20315](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.20315v2)). Grouping the eight samples per item by their *vector* of results across the original database and four copies, with no gold comparison, adds the distinguishing inputs that single-DB voting lacks. The team should compare four rules: first sample, single-DB majority, multi-DB-vector majority, and oracle pass@8. A related use goes straight to the 61–65% silent-error rates. When an item's samples split into several result-vector clusters, the answer can be flagged as unreliable. The team can report that flag's precision and recall for catching silent errors. A deployable "this answer is shaky" signal for a small business-SQL agent is a strong answer to the "practical usefulness" criterion. One honesty constraint applies. The copies were generated with knowledge of the gold query's bug families, so the result-vector grouping is gold-free but the copy construction is not. A fully deployable claim needs copies regenerated from the schema alone, using generic operators: duplicate and delete rows, inject NULLs, create ties on ORDER BY/LIMIT columns, and shift values across WHERE thresholds. Report the gold-informed version as an analysis and the schema-only version as the deployable one.

## Under $10 of L4 time buys a seed check and a regime check

The open question judges will press on is whether C < B reflects the reward or the run. One more seed per arm costs about $2.4–2.6 each (3.2–3.5 h at $0.74/h) and answers the coarse version. If seed 2 reproduces a gap of similar sign and size, the reward is doing something, and the replay explains what. If the gap shrinks or flips, the honest statement becomes "within run-to-run variance at n = 2". Two seeds cannot deliver statistical power. At an assumed seed SD of 1–2 points, detecting a 3-point difference at 80% power needs roughly 5–10 seeds per arm (derived from the standard two-sample formula). Miller's evals guidance still applies at the item level: use paired, item-clustered comparisons ([arXiv 2411.00640](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2411.00640)). If intermediate checkpoints were saved (steps 30 and 45), evaluating them gives a nearly free proxy for trajectory noise.

The second purchase is a calibration run that finds a regime where the single-DB verifier is genuinely weak. The current training databases sit where the two verifiers mostly agree. Sampling the SFT model eight times per item on small-slice originals (around 50 customers) measures how many original-DB passes become lucky, and how many groups have mixed outcomes. This costs about an hour of generation by the notes' extrapolation; measure throughput on 50 prompts first. Offline prefiltering to items with 1–7 passes out of 8 approximates DAPO's dynamic sampling, which drops zero-variance groups so every batch carries gradient ([arXiv 2503.14476](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.14476v1)). Reinforce-Rej shows that this filtering alone matches GRPO with better stability ([arXiv 2504.11343](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2504.11343)). If even small slices yield few lucky passes for Tiny Aya, that is itself a finding: the stricter verifier cannot matter for this model at this accuracy. If they yield 25–30%, the shortlist window has a regime where a min or penalty reward should separate from B.

If the budget allows, a cheap generalisation check is to run a few Apache-2.0 SQL specialists on the team's synthetic test set with vLLM on the L4: Arctic-Text2SQL-R1-7B, OmniSQL-7B, Qwen2.5-Coder-7B and XiYanSQL-7B ([HF API](https://huggingface.co/api/models/Snowflake/Arctic-Text2SQL-R1-7B)). Report each model's single-DB accuracy, verified accuracy and lucky-pass share. That would show whether lucky passes persist in RL-trained SQL models, not just Tiny Aya and Command A. The notes estimate $1.5–2.2 for this sweep, but nobody has benchmarked it on an L4. If the Spider rescoring lands, this item becomes optional.

## Lead with the audit, own the aggregator, and show the before-after

Lexsi scores on four criteria: write-up clarity, practical usefulness, code/notebook quality and overall review. Judging is double-blind with several judges per team ([Lexsi kickoff slides](https://hackathon.lexsi.ai/kickoff-slides/)). The rules require "before-and-after metrics (more than one metric)" and an explanation of "how the resulting model is meaningfully better for usage". They also say an ROI comparison against a closed model is preferred ([hackathon.lexsi.ai](https://hackathon.lexsi.ai/)). The team now satisfies the before-and-after rule outright. Verified accuracy goes base 0.9% → SFT 9.4% → GRPO-B 12.7% (SFT vs base p < 1e-5, B vs SFT p = 0.006). The other required metrics are already in hand: silent-error rate (65.4% → 64.0%), lucky-pass rate (~5.1% for all three), and cost. The ROI line should be stated as cost per *verified-correct* answer, not per query. Command A works out to $4.25/0.81 ≈ $5.25 per 1,000 correct answers and Tiny Aya B to $0.06/0.127 ≈ $0.47, so the advantage is roughly **11×, not 70×**, alongside a large accuracy gap a judge will see anyway. Problem #6 asks for agents "rewarded on verified results", and #9 for rewards that "raise real task success without being gamed" ([Lexsi kickoff slides](https://hackathon.lexsi.ai/kickoff-slides/)). The audit fully answers the "without being gamed" half. The B-over-SFT gain and the test-time selection result supply evidence for the "raise task success" half. Training with the counterfactual reward does not, and the write-up should say so in its TL;DR.

The null should be presented the way strong audit papers present surprises. METR's reward-hacking report pairs each named hack with a code excerpt, counts and percentages, and transcript links. It recommends patching scorers rather than training against detected hacks ([METR](https://metr.org/blog/2025-06-05-recent-reward-hacking)). Anthropic found that removing viable hacks from environments helped, while filtering hacking examples did not ([arXiv 2511.18397](https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.18397)). That makes "we closed the hacks in the reward" a safety contribution in its own right. Spurious Rewards turned an unexpected RL outcome into a finding by measuring a behavioural mechanism and ablating it ([PMLR v306](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v306/shao26d.html)). The team's analogue is the replay figure. It shows rollouts by disagreement type (lucky vs lenient) and the advantage-sign flips under mean, min and penalty aggregation. The recommended wording: "H4/H5 not supported. C scored 3.7 points below B on fresh test databases (95% CI [−5.3, −2.3]). With one training seed per arm we cannot yet separate a reward effect from run variance; seed 2 shows [result]. The mean aggregator gave partial credit to queries wrong on the visible database, and under group normalisation that partly offset its strictness. That is a design error we document so others avoid it." Do not call the gap noise, and do not call C "stricter" until the replay confirms the split.

The audit contains a finding in the organisers' own library: AgentTune's SQL reward is gameable by WHERE-padding and always-"yes" queries. That finding fits Lexsi's culture. Their kickoff slides advertise an "alignment audit: reward hacking" feature, and AgentTune keeps a public Known Issues page with upstream fixes ([agenttune.lexsi.ai](https://agenttune.lexsi.ai)). Include a minimal repro, a patch and a regression test. Name both AgentTune and AuditKIT with the features used, because the slides and the main site disagree on whether Track 3 is exempt from the two-repo rule ([hackathon.lexsi.ai](https://hackathon.lexsi.ai/)). Put the Zhong et al. citation and the novelty delta near the top. Put the open questions in a threats-to-validity box: one seed, one model, synthetic schemas, and gold-informed copies. Use checklist section 8 ("With more credits: your approach, before and after results") for a pre-registered prediction that the shortlist window will test.

## Conclusion

The project's real result is narrower than "counterfactual rewards help", and more interesting. The audit shows that a verifier can be far more accurate on adversarial probes while barely changing on-policy training signal. The aggregation rule decides whether the extra databases tighten the reward or quietly loosen it. If the replay confirms that most of the 7% disagreement was lenient credit, the team holds a mechanistic explanation of its own null, which few hackathon entries will have. It also gets a clean, cheap retest: min or penalty aggregation on prompts where the single database is actually fooled. If the replay shows the disagreement was mostly lucky passes after all, the C < B gap becomes a sharper puzzle that a second seed must arbitrate. Either way, the pre-Nov 14 work should move the core claim from "our synthetic setup" to "public benchmarks, public RL stacks and public predictions". That is what double-blind judges can verify without trusting the team.

The deeper implication for Track 3 is a cheap pre-training diagnostic. Before spending GPU hours on a new reward, measure on-policy disagreement *by type* and the advantage-sign flips it causes. The team's run is one data point that a 7% raw disagreement can contain almost no stricter-verifier signal. Stated as a hypothesis to test in the shortlist window, with a pre-registered threshold, it gives judges a falsifiable thesis rather than an apology.
